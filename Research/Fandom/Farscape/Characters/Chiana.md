# Chiana

> First-pass dossier. This note covers television canon and production evidence located in this research pass; it is not a complete Chiana bibliography, scene index, or continuity audit.

## Canonical identity

Chiana is a female [[Nebari]] who becomes a member of [[Moya]]'s fugitive crew. She is first encountered as a young prisoner being transported by Nebari authority Salis in **1.15, “Durka Returns.”** The episode establishes her as a survivor of Nebari state control and as someone willing to use deception and violence to remain free. The production episode guide credits Gigi Edgley as Chiana in this episode and identifies the character as recurring from this point, becoming a regular by episode 21 in the guide's cast accounting. [1]

- Species: Nebari.
- Gender: female.
- Affiliation: Moya's crew; her history also intersects with the Nebari resistance.
- Family documented on-screen: Nerri, her brother.
- Typical equipment: pulse pistol; she also demonstrates piloting and improvisational survival skills.
- First appearance: **1.15, “Durka Returns.”**
- Last television appearance in this scope: **Farscape: The Peacekeeper Wars** (2004).
- Performer: **Gigi Edgley**. Claudia Black plays Aeryn Sun, not Chiana. [1][2][3]

Age is not established here as a firm canon fact. Fan references sometimes assign ages or infer a precise chronology; those claims remain unverified in this pass.

## Character arc

### From controlled prisoner to chosen family

Chiana enters the story under Nebari authority, facing mental cleansing and the destruction of dissent as a threat to her identity. Her first episode with Moya is consequently both a rescue narrative and a test of whether the crew can tolerate a person whose survival ethics are opaque to them. Her refusal to answer cleanly for Salis's murder establishes the character's central ambiguity: she may be dangerous, but the narrative does not reduce danger to a stable moral essence. Rockne S. O'Bannon later described the ambiguity in the episode's closing beat as deliberate characterization rather than a puzzle with a required answer. [5]

Over time, she moves from opportunistic passenger to emotionally invested crewmate. She remains a thief, liar, flirt, gambler, and improviser, but repeatedly risks herself for the crew, Moya, and the people she has chosen. This is an arc of attachment without domestication: Chiana does not become conventionally respectable; she becomes more capable of loyalty and of recognizing the cost of her choices.

### Grief, freedom, and self-determination

“Taking the Stone” makes the death of her brother Nerri the major early rupture in her life on Moya. Her attempt to disappear into a youth community frames grief as both a desire for oblivion and a demand to control her own response to loss. Later stories return to her fear of being defined by other people's institutions, expectations, or romantic plans.

Her relationship with D'Argo exposes this conflict most clearly. Their intimacy can be read as one of the first relationships in which Chiana is not simply trading sex, information, or access for survival. Yet the relationship breaks when D'Argo's imagined future requires her to become a role—wife, farmer, and participant in his dream—rather than remain a self-determining person. Their friendship survives, and D'Argo's death and the transfer of his Qualta blade to Jothee leave Chiana carrying both grief and responsibility.

### Series regular and tonal counterforce

Production evidence indicates that Chiana was initially expected to die in “Durka Returns”; the bullet was changed to a graze after the character's reception, and she was then retained. [2][6] Her expanded role lets the series place an openly sensual, criminalized, anti-authoritarian survivor beside characters whose own histories are framed through military, religious, royal, or scientific institutions.

## Major relationships

- [[Ka D'Argo]]: intimacy, conflict over autonomy and future-planning, eventual durable friendship; his death is one of Chiana's defining losses.
- [[Ka Jothee]]: attraction and an affair that intensify the collapse of Chiana's relationship with D'Argo; the episode material should be revisited for consent, coercion, and perspective rather than summarized only as a plot twist.
- [[John Crichton]]: flirtation and sexual tension coexist with wary affection and a sibling-like bond; Chiana's attraction is not reciprocated as a primary romance because Crichton's central relationship is with Aeryn.
- [[Aeryn Sun]]: rivalry, jealousy, verbal antagonism, and genuine care. Their relationship gradually reveals mutual recognition between two survivors of violent institutions.
- [[Zotoh Zhaan]]: an imperfect maternal or spiritual attachment. Chiana's need for care and Zhaan's limited availability create both tenderness and frustration.
- [[Rygel XVI]]: shared cunning and theft create affinity, but their motives differ: Chiana tends toward thrill, improvisation, and survival; Rygel toward status and acquisitive self-interest.
- [[Moya]] and [[Pilot]]: the living ship and its Pilot become part of the home Chiana chooses, not merely the transport that happens to contain her.
- [[Jool]]: hostility gradually becomes practical partnership and mutual respect.
- [[Sikozu]] and [[Noranti]]: later-crew relationships are less fully dramatized in the available first-pass evidence; Chiana's suspicion of Sikozu and working cooperation with Noranti merit episode-level review.

## Abilities and cultural context

Canonically demonstrated or strongly supported in the screen material and episode guides:

- social manipulation, lying, theft, bargaining, and improvisational survival;
- proficiency with a pulse pistol and willingness to use violence;
- piloting a Peacekeeper Prowler and other craft;
- sexual self-presentation as a survival and bargaining tool, without treating that history as her whole identity;
- practical knowledge of the Uncharted Territories and of how to read dangerous social situations;
- unusual physical agility, including a dramatic leap in “Mind the Baby.” Whether the leap is a species trait, an emotional feat, or a one-off gag is unresolved and should not be generalized without episode evidence.

The Nebari context is authoritarian and assimilationist: “mind cleansing” is used to remove dissent, and Chiana's flight is therefore an escape from compulsory ideological conformity as much as from physical custody. “A Clockwork Nebari” is the essential episode for testing what the series actually establishes about Nebari politics, resistance, contagion, family, and the limits of Chiana's knowledge. Planned worldbuilding notes: [[Nebari]], [[Nebari Prime]], [[Nebari Resistance]], and [[Mind Cleansing]].

## Performer and production credits

Chiana is performed by Australian actor **Gigi Edgley**. The archived BBC actor profile identifies her as a drama-college graduate whose five-hour makeup test preceded her casting, and records that she advocated for Chiana's first-season mannerisms to return in season four. [2]

## Costume, makeup, movement, and performance

The strongest accessible production account is Edgley and Elsey's interview in *The Companion*, supplemented by the archived BBC profile and the public Jim Henson Company video link embedded by that article. [2][5][7]

- Early Chiana concept art reportedly used white/orange facial tones and a design later considered too graphic when translated into straight makeup. The design was adjusted around Edgley's face.
- A test version included rat-like prosthetic teeth, but the teeth were abandoned because they impeded speech and caused dribbling.
- The final look used silver hair, broken-up grey/white facial tones, and large dark contact lenses. The lenses restricted peripheral vision, which contributed to Chiana's pronounced head turns.
- Edgley describes an approximately three-and-a-half-hour makeup process and a very early call time. A separate contemporary account reports a five-hour initial test; these figures likely describe different stages, but the distinction needs production documentation before being reconciled.
- The first costume for “Durka Returns” was built to preserve movement while maintaining mystery; later versions had to be altered because Edgley's crouched “Chiana position” exposed too much of the costume.
- Edgley developed an animalistic head cock, crouched posture, breath, and non-human physical vocabulary. Her stated goal was not to play a human wearing alien makeup, but an alien from the Uncharted Territories.
- The voice changed during development: the interview reports an initial American direction, an Australian direction by the end of season one, later pressure to redub toward American delivery, and a return to the voice Edgley originally found from season two onward. This should be verified against episode audio before treating accent shifts as continuity.
- The opening episode's threat is performed through withheld information, a smile, and physical stillness after kinetic movement. This is a performance construction, not evidence that the canon has a single hidden answer to who killed Salis.

## Canon versus analysis

### Canon / production evidence

Chiana is Nebari; Gigi Edgley performs her; she first appears in “Durka Returns”; she joins Moya's crew; Nerri is her brother; she appears across the four seasons and in *The Peacekeeper Wars*; Nebari mind cleansing and her history with the Nebari resistance are central to her story. Episode-level appearance and credit claims are cited to the episode guide and fan index, with the fan index treated as a lead rather than a sole authority. [1][2][3]

### Analysis

Chiana's durable dramatic function is to make freedom look unruly. The series repeatedly asks whether a person can escape coercive institutions without becoming legible to a more respectable institution. Her sexuality, criminality, and mobility are not merely rebellious decoration: they are survival practices that the crew must learn to interpret without either romanticizing or disciplining her. Her arc is therefore less “becoming good” than acquiring chosen obligations while resisting possession by anyone else's story.

The grey/black-and-white visual design supports a productive refusal of moral color coding. That reading is analysis, not an explicit production statement. It should be tested against costume, lighting, and makeup interviews rather than asserted as creator intent.
